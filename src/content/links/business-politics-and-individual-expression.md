John Gruber has been clear-eyed in his coverage of Jensen Huang’s departure from NVIDIA and the events that preceded it. Here he reflects on early-2026 comments from Huang about President Trump and what they reveal about the extent to which Huang was hitching himself and NVIDIA to Trump:

> Ever since the Huang/Nvidia scandal erupted, I’ve been thinking about a short post I wrote back in March, under the headline “Jensen Huang Doesn’t Smell Anything”:
>
>> Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, during an on-stage interview at The Hill & Valley Forum last summer, was asked “What do you see as America’s unique advantages that other countries don’t have?”
>>
>> His answer, after taking a moment to think, “America’s unique advantage that no country could possibly have is President Trump”
>
> […] If you’re not paying close attention you might think Huang’s was the sort of answer any major tech CEO might have given. But it’s not. That was a really unusual thing for Huang to say, way out of line with how corporate leaders typically approach politics.

He goes on to draw a contrast with Tim Cook, who managed to win favour with Trump without venturing into politics. 

This paragraph, though, captured something I’ve been wrestling with as I’ve started to write in public this year (emphasis mine):

> This is what we should want: business leaders who endeavor to stay out of partisan politics, not hopelessly enmesh themselves in it. Industry and politics cannot operate in isolation, but they should avoid intertwinement except where necessary. Great companies stand the test of time. That’s not feasible if you hitch your corporate wagon to one party or the other, no matter the political moment. We don’t think of Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola, or Berkshire Hathaway as being Democratic or Republican companies. You cannot chart their fortunes over time based on which party was in power in any given decade. *You may well have strong political opinions that are far from the 50-yard line, on one side or the other. I do. But you shouldn’t look for those opinions to be validated by industry executives*, any more than you should look for business and product-design decisions to be made by elected government officials.

Gruber is focusing on the line between politics and business, and I wholeheartedly agree with him. A for-profit business exists to create value and—if it is well-run—generate a financial return for its shareholders. A business is also run by employees of all stripes, each of whom has their own political views. When a business strays too far into politics, it runs the risk of alienating its own employees, and placing itself in a difficult position in terms of public perception when the political winds inevitably shift. 

What Gruber doesn’t touch on is how an individual should straddle the same line, especially when they have a professional reputation to uphold. 

Consider my situation. I am not an independent voice like Gruber, writing for a living, but I am writing under my own name, in public. I also haven’t escaped the need to have an employer. This puts me in a difficult position when I approach writing. What can I write about with no regrets? How strong can my opinions be? Where are the land mines buried for me? 

I have many unfinished drafts where I have expressed an opinion about a company, a public figure, or a current event only to self-censor prior to publishing. My concern stems from the potential for future employers or clients to read what I write and take issue with my perspective. If I critique a company publicly, my chances of them hiring or working with me in the future narrow. This matters when the area you want to write about is also the area you work in. 

One of the reasons I find the majority of LinkedIn posts intolerable is that the writing is usually vapid, self-congratulatory and devoid of real opinion about specific companies, products, or public figures. Everyone who needs a job is trying to keep their options open, so nobody really speaks their mind. It’s all performative. To get the truth from these people, you need to speak to them in person. It’s a shame, because many people only post on LinkedIn, and therefore their entire public profile has been whittled down to a thin veneer of their true selves. 

When I think about the voices I encounter each day that actually resonate with me, it’s some combination of professional journalists, analysts, independent creators, and business owners—people who are either paid to speak candidly, or can afford to do so. For the rest of us, there is a chilling effect because of our economic dependence on employers. This bothers me. 

My reasons for writing are straightforward. I’ve wanted to write for years, it’s a skill I want to master, and I believe that a corpus of writing will be a net benefit to me over the long term. Whether I can overcome my own self-censorship remains to be seen and is probably linked to several other situational factors in my life.
